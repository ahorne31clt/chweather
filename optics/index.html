WHAT THE MODEL SEES

Not just “high clouds.”

Hourly HRRR low/middle/high cloud and visibility fields replace the corresponding GFS fields where available. GFS provides temperature, humidity, cloud ice, and rain; this is explicitly a hybrid, not a full HRRR microphysics forecast. The ice-optics recipe combines high-cloud fraction with temperature, relative humidity, and cloud-ice mixing ratio at 300, 400, and 500 hPa. Low/middle clouds, visibility, and a simplified sunward viewing path reduce the score when sunlight may be obscured.

Rainbows use a different recipe: modeled liquid-rain ingredients in a sector 10–40 km opposite the Sun, an illuminated viewing location, and suitable solar elevation. Rain directly overhead alone is not enough.

Pressure levels are not fixed heights. Relative humidity is used as published by GFS, rather than relabeled as ice supersaturation. Neither high-cloud cover nor ice mixing ratio measures the cloud’s optical depth or proves that crystals have the required shape.

The model cannot distinguish the crystal orientations needed for a halo versus an upper tangent arc, so those views intentionally share their ingredient score. Their explanations describe different optical mechanisms, not independent event predictions.

Observe safely. Never look directly at the Sun or scan near it with binoculars or a telescope. Keep the solar disk safely hidden behind a solid obstruction. Do not approach thunderstorms to look for rainbows.

REFRACTION / ICE

22° halos & sundogs

Hexagonal ice crystals bend sunlight. A 22° halo can form with varied crystal orientations; sundogs favor horizontally oriented plates and are often prominent with a low Sun. The “22°” describes angular distance, not cloud height or air temperature.

SUN BELOW ~32°

Circumzenithal arc

A bright arc near the zenith, made by plate-shaped ice crystals. The geometry requires a low Sun; the heuristic gives greatest geometric support near 22° solar elevation. Cloud suitability is still required.

SUN ABOVE ~58°

Circumhorizontal arc

A broad colored arc below the Sun, parallel to the horizon. Plate-crystal refraction requires a very high Sun, so this display is geometrically impossible in many locations and seasons. It is not a “fire rainbow.”

COLUMN CRYSTALS

Upper tangent arc

Horizontally oriented ice columns can produce an arc touching the top of the 22° halo. Its shape changes as the Sun rises. Crystal alignment is unresolved, preventing a distinct occurrence forecast.

SUN BEHIND YOU

Rainbows

Sunlight refracts and reflects in liquid droplets opposite the Sun. A primary bow has a radius near 42° around the antisolar point. For an observer on level ground, the Sun generally needs to be below about 42°; elevated viewpoints can differ.

RESEARCH / VERIFY

Keep a sky journal.

Record the UTC time, location, phenomenon, approximate solar elevation, cloud appearance, and a safely framed photo. Record non-detections too. This prototype has no measured forecast skill; observations are needed before its scores can be calibrated.
