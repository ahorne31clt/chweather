READ THE SIGNAL

A season is not a daily forecast.

Seasonal maps describe the average pattern across three months. A warmer season can still contain cold spells, and a drier season can still bring flooding.

ENSO / OBSERVED ANALOGS

Historical context.

The pure ENSO comparison uses the closest historical ONI matches from the same time of year, with one match per qualifying El Niño or La Niña episode. The blend uses a broader climate/state-conditioned historical sample. ONI is used for the historical selection; the ENSO tracker separately follows CPC’s operational RONI monitoring.

THE METHOD / VERSION 3

A blend with a traceable recipe.

01 / DYNAMICAL GUIDANCE

Six model components.

CFSv2, CanESM5, GEM5.2-NEMO, NASA GEOS, NCAR CCSM4, and NCAR CESM1 each enter once. The NMME aggregate is retained as a comparison, rather than counted as a seventh model.

02 / OBSERVED STATE

History with context.

The statistical stream matches historical ENSO level and tendency, AO, NAO, PNA, and recent temperature/precipitation departures. PDO, QBO, Pacific ENSO structure, issued ENSO forecasts, and temperature trend adjustments are additional candidates, selected only when earlier-year tests support them. Regional weights and predictor choices are tested on later winters excluded from tuning, with small-sample shrinkage.

03 / SNOWFALL GUIDANCE

Broad ranges, wider uncertainty.

Measured historical snowfall is conditioned on the blended temperature/precipitation pattern and observed state. The amount map places its central estimate in a broad band such as 6–12 inches. That band is not a confidence interval; the city view shows a wider historical scenario range.

Sources, coverage, and limitations

All CH Weather Blend fields are experimental. The model/statistical mixture is distinct from the official CPC outlook. Outer test winters are excluded from weight and predictor selection. Limited winters, fixed published normals, and changing operational model versions still limit the results. Archived NMME membership, model versions, and climatologies changed during the development period.

Above/below-average blend maps describe an experimental outcome distribution relative to the arithmetic 1991–2020 average. NMME comparison probabilities retain the publisher’s above/near/below terciles, which are different thresholds. Pure ENSO percentages remain historical frequencies.

Long-record cooperative stations supplement the original city network. All three target months must be present and at least 24 normal seasons are required. Zero-snow climates remain visible rather than being removed. Broad regional contours use linear interpolation and approximately 120 km smoothing on a 0.2° display grid; that spacing does not imply 0.2° predictive skill. Coverage metadata reports the fraction of land with edge extrapolation or weaker nearby observational support. Snow amount and anomaly surfaces are anchored to NOAA’s denser 1991–2020 snowfall-normal stations, with the blend’s relative departure applied over that baseline. This reduces mountain-to-desert spillover; it is an interpolated normal surface, not an official gridded snowfall forecast. City readouts use their own complete-case station averages and can differ from the mapped normal surface. Mountains and local lake effects remain poorly resolved.

Where a long-record station lacks recent observations, nearby current records supply the recent-condition anomaly. The model and statistical anomalies are combined directly using the shared stream weights. No short-archive station offset is added, and the uncertainty distribution is centered so it cannot shift the mean again. This extends the surface without pretending each station has a complete validation record.

AO, NAO, and PNA are observed snapshots. They are not assumed to keep their current sign for the upcoming season. Their similarity weight decreases with forecast lead. PDO, QBO, Pacific ENSO structure and issued ENSO forecasts are tested as additional predictors; missing sources remain unavailable. The source months and values are shown above.

Snowfall uses an observed historical distribution, with climatological shrinkage to avoid overconfidence. Snow amount bands classify the central estimate; the wider 10th–90th percentile scenario range is not independently validated for 80% forecast coverage. Individual storm totals and high-elevation conditions can differ greatly.
